\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_protocol}

\noindent\textbf{Hardware, tasks, and data collection.}
The evaluation includes $5$ single-arm and $4$ bimanual tasks on two platforms (Table~\ref{tab:tasks}). The single-arm platform is a 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda with wrist-mounted and external Intel RealSense D405 cameras. Demonstrations are collected using a 3Dconnexion SpaceMouse. The bimanual platform is an X Square Robot with one wrist-mounted camera per arm and one shared external camera. Demonstrations are collected through leader--follower teleoperation. Following ForceVLA~\cite{yu2025forcevlaenhancingvlamodels}, both platforms use robot-provided wrench estimates without additional wrist force/torque sensors.

For each task, we collect $200$ demonstrations. Multi-view images, robot states, and commanded actions are recorded at $30$~Hz, and timestamped wrench estimates at $100$~Hz.

\noindent\textbf{Baselines and evaluation protocol.}
We select baselines to cover complementary comparisons. $\pi_{0.5}$~\cite{intelligence2025pi05visionlanguageactionmodelopenworld} represents a general force-agnostic VLA. ForceVLA~\cite{yu2025forcevlaenhancingvlamodels}, which also underlies our teacher, provides the direct force-aware full-action baseline. TA-VLA~\cite{zhang2025tavlaelucidatingdesignspace} represents an alternative torque-aware VLA design. ImplicitRDP~\cite{chen2026implicitrdpendtoendvisualforcediffusion} provides a strong end-to-end slow--fast visual--force comparison outside the VLA family. Temporal Teacher directly rolls out the Stage-1 teacher and isolates residual distillation from temporal force conditioning. The controlled comparison between fast full-action generation and reference-anchored residual prediction is provided by the architecture-matched Fast full-action distillation ablation in Sec.~\ref{sec:exp_ablation}. All methods use the same task demonstrations and are evaluated under the same initial-state distributions, controller settings, and safety limits. For each method and task, we conduct $20$ evaluation trials. We report success rate, peak contact force, and completion time. Peak force is computed over all trials as $\max_t\|\mathbf f(t)\|_2$ for single-arm tasks and $\max_{t,a\in\{L,R\}}\|\mathbf f_a(t)\|_2$ for bimanual tasks. Completion time is computed over successful trials.

\noindent\textbf{Implementation details.}
Both platforms execute policy commands through Cartesian impedance controllers with proportional--derivative feedback. For safety, Cartesian commands are issued at a maximum rate of $100$~Hz on both platforms. The temporal force-conditioned teacher is fully fine-tuned using $8$ NVIDIA A100 GPUs. Real-robot deployment uses a workstation equipped with $2$ NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs with 24~GB of memory each.

\label{sec:exp_main}

\noindent\textbf{Qualitative results.}
Figure~\ref{fig:qualitative_button} shows a representative button-pressing rollout with synchronized front- and wrist-camera observations, robot-estimated end-effector force components, and translational residual magnitude. The residual grows as sustained contact begins, peaks near the force transient, and decreases during release, illustrating its contact-responsive local behavior.

\noindent\textbf{Task performance.}
ForceDelta-VLA achieves an average success rate of $82.2\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}), compared with $47.2\%$, $54.4\%$, and $57.2\%$ for $\pi_{0.5}$, ForceVLA, and TA-VLA, $72.8\%$ for ImplicitRDP, and $68.9\%$ for the Temporal Teacher. The largest differences from ImplicitRDP appear on the insertion and removal tasks: USB insertion ($80\%$ vs. $55\%$), plug insertion ($70\%$ vs. $50\%$), and plug removal ($80\%$ vs. $65\%$). The methods tie on object flipping ($90\%$), button pressing ($85\%$), and single-arm whiteboard wiping ($90\%$); on these three tasks no method in Table~\ref{tab:main_results} exceeds the shared value, so they separate the stronger methods only weakly.

The baseline progression separates temporal force modeling from residual execution. The Temporal Teacher, which replaces ForceVLA's instantaneous force encoding with temporal force history, reaches $68.9\%$ mean success, compared with $54.4\%$ for ForceVLA; independent residual execution reaches $82.2\%$. ForceDelta-VLA also has the lowest mean successful-trial completion time on both platforms: $23.9$/$25.8$~s, compared with $27.8$/$29.2$~s for the Temporal Teacher and $25.8$/$27.9$~s for ImplicitRDP. These correspond to reductions of $14.0\%/11.6\%$ relative to the Temporal Teacher and $7.4\%/7.5\%$ relative to ImplicitRDP. Thus, the gains in success and contact force are not accompanied by longer mean completion time among successful trials. The runtime and ablation results below examine the contribution of frequent reference-anchored correction.

\noindent\textbf{Contact quality.}
ForceDelta-VLA yields the lowest platform-aggregated peak contact force among all methods (Table~\ref{tab:interaction}). Relative to the Temporal Teacher, peak force decreases from $15.1$ to $11.8$~N on single-arm tasks and from $14.7$ to $12.5$~N on bimanual tasks, reductions of $21.9\%$ and $15.0\%$. Relative to ImplicitRDP, the reductions are $15.7\%$ and $18.8\%$, respectively. The reduction is observed on both platforms alongside higher task success. Peak force is the quantity most directly affected by the residual pathway: a correction issued within milliseconds of a contact transient bounds the commanded pose before the interaction force develops.

\noindent\textbf{Qualitative failure modes.}
Inspection of failed ForceDelta-VLA rollouts reveals three recurring and potentially overlapping limitations. First, an invalid task-level reference can move the trajectory outside the bounded residual's correction envelope. Second, excessive reference age beyond the training schedule can make the cached local plan inconsistent with the evolving robot--environment state. Third, jamming or wrong-side contact can require nonlocal recovery---such as retreat, re-approach, or strategy switching---that cannot be expressed by a local pose correction. These overlapping failure modes delineate the locality boundary of reference-anchored residual execution.

\subsection{Independent Residual Execution}
\label{sec:exp_runtime}

Two experiments assess independent residual execution. Runtime profiling quantifies the realized rate separation between the reference and residual paths; reference-delay evaluation tests whether this separation improves contact behavior as the cached reference becomes stale.

\noindent\textbf{Realized rate separation.}
Table~\ref{tab:runtime} reports the runtime characteristics of the policy pathways under the same button-pressing deployment setting. The Temporal Teacher and ForceDelta reference require $176.4$ and $189.7$~ms per batch-one inference, corresponding to nominal model throughputs of $5.7$ and $5.3$~Hz. Their realized worker publication rates are lower, at $4.2$ and $3.8$~Hz, because they include end-to-end deployment overhead beyond the isolated forward pass. In contrast, the ForceDelta residual requires $2.43$~ms per inference and publishes at $138.6$~Hz. This confirms the intended multi-rate execution: the reference supplies lower-frequency action guidance, while the lightweight residual supplies high-rate corrections without repeatedly executing the full VLA. The publication rate is not the robot command rate; the $100$-Hz command interface applies the latest valid residual packet at each control update.

\noindent\textbf{Robustness to delayed references.}
We evaluate USB insertion and plug insertion with $0$, $100$, and $200$~ms of additional packet-publication delay, using $10$ trials per task, method, and delay. ForceDelta-VLA degrades more gradually than the Temporal Teacher: from $0$ to $200$~ms, success drops by $15$ versus $25$ percentage points, while peak contact force increases by approximately $30\%$ versus $60\%$. This suggests that independent residual updates improve robustness to delayed reference availability (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_sensitivity}).

\subsection{Ablation Studies}
\label{sec:exp_ablation}

We organize the ablations into two groups (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).
\emph{Execution/component ablations} keep the trained model fixed. \textbf{Reference only} disables both residual outputs; \textbf{w/o force residual} and \textbf{w/o staleness residual} disable the named output. \textbf{Shuffled force history} preserves the empirical force-history marginal but breaks its observation--action correspondence. \textbf{Zeroed residual input} replaces the deployed residual policy's measured force history with an all-zero tensor while retaining the force encoder and residual branch, which suppresses measured force information but also moves the force-encoder input away from the measured-wrench distribution seen in training. The shuffled variant separates these two effects and is therefore the primary force-input diagnostic.

\emph{Supervision/decomposition ablations} retrain the affected variant. \textbf{Demonstration-derived residual target} subtracts the missing-force teacher prediction from the demonstrated action at the same query state. \textbf{Zero-wrench teacher query} replaces the learned missing-force mode during reference and target construction with an all-zero-wrench teacher query. \textbf{w/o schedule replay} omits sampled asynchronous query and packet-latency schedules during residual training. \textbf{Single combined-residual head} predicts the sum of the force and staleness targets with one head. \textbf{Fast full-action distillation} uses the same compact student inputs and update schedule but directly distills the frozen teacher's force-conditioned pose action. These interventions differ from $\pi_{0.5}$, a separately trained VLA with no force-input pathway.

Among the force-input diagnostics, shuffling produces the largest degradation: success falls from $85.0\%/75.0\%$ to $55.0\%/45.0\%$ on the single-arm/bimanual task groups, while peak force rises from $11.7/12.8$~N to $18.4/20.1$~N. Zeroing reduces success to $65.0\%/55.0\%$ and raises peak force to $15.9/17.7$~N. Shuffling preserves the marginal magnitude of the force history but not its correspondence to the observation, so its degradation relative to the full model isolates the temporal alignment of the force input rather than its magnitude.

The supervision/decomposition ablations show that a single combined head reaches $75.0\%/65.0\%$ success with $13.5/14.9$~N peak force, compared with $85.0\%/75.0\%$ and $11.7/12.8$~N for the full model. Removing schedule replay raises peak force to $13.8/15.6$~N. Within the execution group, disabling the staleness residual changes peak force least, to $13.2/14.5$~N, against $15.4/17.1$~N for the force residual, so the staleness pathway contributes less than the force pathway. Head separation and schedule replay each lower peak force. The success-rate differences remain directional with $40$ trials per platform and variant. Fast full-action distillation yields $67.5\%/52.5\%$ success and $15.2/17.9$~N peak force; it replaces both the residual parameterization and the reference anchoring, so it bounds their joint contribution without separating them. Demonstration-derived targets and the zero-wrench teacher query also degrade both metrics. Overall, the clearest effects come from temporally aligned force use and reference-anchored residual prediction.
