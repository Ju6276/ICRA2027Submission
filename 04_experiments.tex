Our experiments address task performance, contact quality, and component contributions. Specifically, we ask whether ForceDelta-VLA improves success over force-free, force-aware, and fast--slow policies; whether the distilled residual improves physical interaction rather than producing more aggressive motion; and which components of the proposed distillation and asynchronous execution account for the improvement.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_protocol}

\subsubsection{Hardware platforms.}
The single-arm platform consists of a seven-degree-of-freedom Franka Emika Panda manipulator observed by two Intel RealSense D405 cameras. \assumed{A six-axis ATI Mini45 sensor is mounted between the wrist and gripper. The bimanual platform comprises two seven-degree-of-freedom Franka Emika Panda manipulators, one wrist force/torque sensor per arm, and two RealSense D405 cameras.} Values and hardware details shown in red are provisional and will be replaced by the final experimental record.

\subsubsection{Tasks and data collection.}
We evaluate the eight tasks in Table~\ref{tab:tasks}. Each task emphasizes a distinct contact regime rather than language or object generalization. We collect $200$ teleoperated demonstrations per single-arm task and $300$ per bimanual task. Multi-view images, robot states, and commanded actions are synchronized at $30$~Hz; timestamped six-axis force/torque measurements are retained at $100$~Hz.

\subsection{Baselines and Metrics}
\label{sec:exp_baselines}

We compare with four representative policies: the force-free $\pi_{0.5}$ VLA~\cite{black2025pi05}; ForceVLA~\cite{yu2025forcevla}, which fuses instantaneous force through a force-aware mixture of experts; TA-VLA~\cite{zhang2025tavla}, which injects temporal torque features into a pretrained VLA; and ImplicitRDP~\cite{chen2025implicitrdp}, a visual--force diffusion policy with structural fast--slow processing.

For each method and task, we evaluate two independently trained seeds with ten trials per seed. The primary metric is success rate, reported per task and averaged separately over the four single-arm and four bimanual tasks. We report peak contact force over all trials and completion time over successful trials as mean $\pm$ standard deviation. Lower values indicate less severe contact and faster successful execution, respectively.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:exp_implementation}

All models are trained on two NVIDIA A100 GPUs using bfloat16 precision. \assumed{The teacher is fully fine-tuned for $50{,}000$ steps with AdamW, a global batch size of $32$, a peak learning rate of $2\times10^{-5}$, weight decay $0.01$, $1{,}000$ warmup steps, cosine decay, and gradient clipping at $1.0$.} The teacher predicts $H{=}50$ actions at $30$~Hz and encodes the latest $100$~ms of the $100$~Hz force/torque stream. \assumed{Flow-matching inference uses $N_{\mathrm{flow}}{=}10$ steps.}

Each arm is controlled by a pose displacement comprising 3D translation and a continuous 6D rotation representation~\cite{zhou2019continuity}, together with a scalar gripper command; bimanual actions concatenate the commands of both arms. Robot states and actions are normalized per channel. The displacement map $\Gamma$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:staleness_target} follows the same pose convention and converts state displacement to normalized action coordinates using the training-set statistics.

After freezing the teacher, \assumed{we optimize the null token and rank-$32$ null-specific adapter for $10{,}000$ steps at a learning rate of $10^{-4}$. The visual--language prefix is pooled into $M{=}16$ tokens. The residual policy contains a causal force encoder and one pre-norm attention block with separate force and staleness output projections. It predicts $K{=}5$ corrections and is trained for $30{,}000$ steps with AdamW, a global batch size of $128$, a learning rate of $3\times10^{-4}$, and $\beta{=}0.5$.} During student training, the cached-reference age is sampled by replaying the asynchronous schedule described in Sec.~\ref{sec:method}.

\subsubsection{Qualitative analysis.}
\assumed{On USB and plug insertion, the null-mode reference brings the connector to the target, after which the residual suppresses lateral motion and introduces small reorientation commands following asymmetric contact. During whiteboard wiping, the residual maintains surface contact despite changes in board pose. For cabinet and drawer opening, repeated corrections along the constrained direction compensate for hinge or rail friction without changing the reference strategy. In free-space segments, the predicted force-conditioned correction remains close to zero.} These observations will be illustrated by synchronized rollout frames, force traces, references, and residuals in the final qualitative figure.

\subsubsection{Failure analysis.}
\assumed{ForceDelta-VLA fails in $20$ of $160$ trials. Eight failures originate from an incorrect reference, including missed pre-contact alignment; seven involve deviations beyond the range of the bounded pose residual; and five require a discrete recovery strategy, such as withdrawing and regrasping.} The first category is inherited from the teacher, whereas the latter categories expose the representational limit of an additive continuous correction.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}
\label{sec:exp_ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} isolates four design choices. The demonstration-derived target replaces the paired teacher difference with the demonstrated action minus the null reference. The force-representation ablation replaces native-rate causal history with the latest force sample. The null-mode variants test whether a physical zero or a token alone provides an adequate reference. Finally, the asynchronous variants remove either the staleness target or schedule replay during training.
