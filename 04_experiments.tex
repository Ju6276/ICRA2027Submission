\subsection{Evaluation Questions}
\label{sec:exp_objectives}

The evaluation addresses four questions: (Q1) Does conditional-residual supervision improve task success and interaction safety over full-action fast branches? (Q2) Does the learned residual depend on force and concentrate around contact? (Q3) Does higher update rate reduce reaction delay and adverse force transients? (Q4) Does timing randomization improve transfer across slow-policy rates and latencies?

\subsection{Attainable Rate of the High-Rate Path}
\label{sec:exp_latency}

The per-update cost of the two designs is measured on the same device at batch size one. For the cached-prefix baseline the vision--language key/value cache is supplied directly and one action-expert forward pass is timed over $816$ prefix and $51$ suffix tokens, then scaled by the sampler's denoising step count, so that the measurement isolates the incremental cost remaining after caching. For the student, one complete forward pass is timed, including the force encoder and the intent projector.

\subsection{Baselines and Metrics}
\label{sec:exp_baselines}

We compare against three published policies and our own method. $\pi_{0.5}$~\cite{black2025pi05} is a vision--language policy without force input, establishing what visual feedback alone attains on these tasks. ForceVLA~\cite{yu2025forcevla} fuses instantaneous force through a force-aware mixture of experts, and isolates the value of force conditioning without native-rate history or rate separation. TA-VLA~\cite{zhang2025tavla} injects torque into the decoder and summarizes torque history into a single token, and is the closest single-system treatment of force history. All baselines are trained on the same demonstrations and executed at their attainable rates.

Two additional variants isolate our own design. A \emph{cached-prefix fast branch}, in which the teacher's action expert is re-run against the cached prefix in the manner of FAVLA and LIFT~\cite{li2026favla,lv2026lift} at the rate permitted by Table~\ref{tab:latency}, holds the slow--fast structure fixed and varies only what the high-rate branch computes. A \emph{direct slow--fast} variant trains our student architecture from demonstrated actions alone, without paired teacher targets, separating the supervision from the architecture.

Task metrics are success rate and stage-wise completion. Interaction quality is measured by peak force, force impulse, limit violations, and post-disturbance recovery rate. Decomposition quality is measured by the residual error $d(\Delta \hat{A},\Delta A_T^{\mathrm{cond}})$ and the composed error $d(A^{\mathrm{ref}}{+}\Delta \hat{A},A_T^{\mathrm{full}})$, each relative to a zero-residual baseline so that the reported quantity is the fraction of the deviation captured. Errors are computed in physical units on the reconstructed absolute pose rather than on normalized deltas, and reported separately for translation and rotation. Runtime metrics are the slow rate, the fast rate, end-to-end latency, and parameter count.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:exp_implementation}

The teacher extends ForceVLA with a causal temporal force encoder, using LoRA adapters on both the vision--language backbone and the action expert and an action horizon of $H{=}50$ at a $30$~Hz action rate. Its force encoder reads a $100$~ms window from the timestamped stream at $100$~Hz. Null adaptation uses rank $\rho{=}32$ on the pose channels, and target extraction uses $N_{\mathrm{flow}}{=}10$ with the prefix pooled into $M{=}16$ bins.

The student is a single attention block over the condition set of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fast_token_sequence}, emitting $K{=}5$ steps with step decay $\beta{=}0.5$, and reads the same $100$~ms force window. Randomized timing draws the slow rate from $[5,15]$~Hz, the inference latency from $[50,300]$~ms, and per-update jitter from $\pm10$~ms, with the age scale $c$ set to cover that band. Demonstrations are restricted to episodes whose force stream sustains at least $100$~Hz. Remaining hyperparameters are given in the supplementary material.

\subsection{Execution Rate}
\label{sec:exp_rate}

The trained student is held fixed and its execution rate capped at $13$, $30$, $50$, and $100$~Hz, the first three matching the rates that Table~\ref{tab:latency} permits for a cached-prefix action expert, and peak contact force, impulse, and success rate are measured at each. Matched contact events are additionally replayed under three configurations: the slow branch alone, both branches synchronized at the slow rate, and full asynchronous execution. For each, we report the delay from a detected force change to the first corrective command, the maximum force preceding recovery, and the time to return to the reference trajectory. This separates the effect of update rate from the effect of residual correction itself: the latency reduction of Table~\ref{tab:latency} is consequential only insofar as contact outcomes improve with rate.

\subsection{Residual Decomposition Analysis}
\label{sec:exp_decomposition}

We inspect the paired teacher predictions used as targets. For each trajectory, $\lVert\Delta A_T^{\mathrm{cond}}\rVert$ is plotted against measured force and task phase; a useful decomposition exhibits small deviations during unconstrained motion and larger, structured deviations near contact onset, transients, and recovery.

Three quantities characterize the decomposition. The first is the relative magnitude of the conditional residual, $\sigma_C^2/\sigma_R^2$, where $\sigma_C^2 = \mathbb{E}\lVert\Delta A_T^{\mathrm{cond}}\rVert^2$ and $\sigma_R^2 = \mathbb{E}\lVert A^{\mathrm{ref}} - A_T^{\mathrm{null}}\rVert^2$ is the reference error incurred by staleness alone. A ratio near or above unity indicates that the conditional residual is comparable to the staleness error and thus warrants a dedicated branch, whereas a small ratio would indicate that the composed error is dominated by staleness and that increasing the slow rate is the more effective intervention. The second is the student's dependence on force, obtained by comparing its residual-error reduction against that of a force-ablated student; a small gap would indicate that corrections are being inferred from intent, state, and reference rather than from force. The third is the locality of that gap, reported separately for contact strata and free space, since a correction that is genuinely contact-driven should exhibit a larger margin under contact.

We further compare the learned null condition against two alternatives used in prior work, a zero force vector as in zero-padding interventions~\cite{idr2026causal} and random force-token masking, testing the claim of Sec.~\ref{sec:null_decomp} that a learned null mode keeps the teacher in distribution. The teacher-to-demonstration gap $d(A_T^{\mathrm{full}},A^{\mathrm{expert}})$ is reported by translation, rotation, and gripper, as it bounds the accuracy attainable by any student distilled from this teacher.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}
\label{sec:exp_ablation}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Force representation:} instantaneous, history downsampled to the visual rate, and native-rate causal history.
    \item \textbf{Counterfactual construction:} learned null condition, zero-force padding, learned token without the rank-$\rho$ adapter, and independent rather than shared flow noise, the last isolating the claim of Sec.~\ref{sec:teacher_differencing} that noise coupling is necessary for the target to be meaningful.
    \item \textbf{Student context:} removing cached intent, state, the reference token, the time token, or the multi-token force sequence.
    \item \textbf{Timing scheme:} fixed slow rate and latency versus randomized, evaluated at both the trained operating point and unseen rates, to test rate transfer.
    \item \textbf{Chunk emission:} $K{=}1$ versus $K{=}5$ under injected inference stalls.
\end{enumerate}
