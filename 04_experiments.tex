\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_protocol}

\noindent\textbf{Hardware, tasks, and data collection.}
The evaluation includes $5$ single-arm and $4$ bimanual tasks on two platforms (Table~\ref{tab:tasks}). The single-arm platform is a 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda with wrist-mounted and external Intel RealSense D405 cameras. Demonstrations are collected using a 3Dconnexion SpaceMouse. The bimanual platform is an X Square Robot with one wrist-mounted camera per arm and one shared external camera. Demonstrations are collected through leader--follower teleoperation. Following ForceVLA~\cite{yu2025forcevlaenhancingvlamodels}, both platforms use robot-provided wrench estimates without additional wrist force/torque sensors.

For each task, we collect $200$ demonstrations. Multi-view images, robot states, and commanded actions are recorded at $30$~Hz, and timestamped wrench estimates at $100$~Hz.

\noindent\textbf{Baselines and evaluation protocol.}
We select baselines to cover complementary comparisons. $\pi_{0.5}$~\cite{intelligence2025pi05visionlanguageactionmodelopenworld} represents a general force-agnostic VLA. ForceVLA~\cite{yu2025forcevlaenhancingvlamodels}, which also underlies our teacher, provides the direct force-aware full-action baseline. TA-VLA~\cite{zhang2025tavlaelucidatingdesignspace} represents an alternative torque-aware VLA design. ImplicitRDP~\cite{chen2026implicitrdpendtoendvisualforcediffusion} provides a strong end-to-end slow--fast visual--force comparison outside the VLA family. Temporal Teacher directly rolls out the Stage-1 teacher and isolates residual distillation from temporal force conditioning. FAVLA~\cite{li2026favlaforceadaptivefastslowvla} is discussed as the closest fast--slow VLA architecture, but is not included quantitatively because no official implementation was available at the time of evaluation. For each method and task, we conduct $20$ evaluation trials. We report success rate, peak contact force, and completion time. Peak force is computed over all trials as $\max_t\|\mathbf f(t)\|_2$ for single-arm tasks and $\max_{t,a\in\{L,R\}}\|\mathbf f_a(t)\|_2$ for bimanual tasks. Completion time is computed over successful trials.

\noindent\textbf{Implementation details.}
Both platforms execute policy commands through Cartesian impedance controllers with proportional--derivative feedback. For safety, Cartesian commands are issued at a maximum rate of $100$~Hz on both platforms. The temporal force-conditioned teacher is fully fine-tuned using $8$ NVIDIA A100 GPUs. Real-robot deployment uses a workstation equipped with $2$ NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs with 24~GB of memory each.

\noindent\textbf{Qualitative results.}
Figure~\ref{fig:qualitative_button} shows a representative button-pressing rollout with synchronized front- and wrist-camera observations, robot-estimated end-effector force components, and translational residual magnitude. The residual grows as sustained contact begins, peaks near the force transient, and decreases during release, illustrating its contact-responsive local behavior.

\noindent\textbf{Task performance.}
ForceDelta-VLA achieves an average success rate of $82.2\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}), compared with $47.2\%$, $54.4\%$, and $57.2\%$ for $\pi_{0.5}$, ForceVLA, and TA-VLA, $72.8\%$ for ImplicitRDP, and $68.9\%$ for the architecture-matched Temporal Teacher. Gains over ImplicitRDP concentrate on geometrically constrained contact tasks: USB insertion ($80\%$ vs. $55\%$), plug insertion ($70\%$ vs. $50\%$), and plug removal ($80\%$ vs. $65\%$). The methods tie on object flipping ($90\%$), button pressing ($85\%$), and single-arm whiteboard wiping ($90\%$). The concentration of gains on insertion and removal matches the intended role of high-rate local pose correction.

The baseline progression separates temporal force modeling from residual execution. Temporal force history raises ForceVLA's mean success rate from $54.4\%$ to $68.9\%$, and independent residual execution reaches $82.2\%$. Relative to the Temporal Teacher, ForceDelta-VLA reduces successful-trial completion time by $19.2\%$ on single-arm tasks and $21.7\%$ on bimanual tasks; it is also slightly faster than ImplicitRDP on both platforms. The runtime and ablation results below examine the contribution of frequent reference-anchored correction.

\noindent\textbf{Contact quality.}
ForceDelta-VLA yields the lowest platform-aggregated peak contact force among all methods (Table~\ref{tab:interaction}). Relative to the Temporal Teacher, peak force decreases from $15.3$ to $11.6$~N on single-arm tasks and from $16.1$ to $12.3$~N on bimanual tasks, reductions of $24.2\%$ and $23.6\%$. Relative to ImplicitRDP, the reductions are approximately $19\%$ on both platforms. ForceDelta-VLA also shortens successful-trial completion time.

\noindent\textbf{Failure analysis.}
Across the $180$ ForceDelta-VLA trials in Table~\ref{tab:main_results}, the $32$ failures comprise reference failure ($14/32$, $43.8\%$), excessive reference staleness ($7/32$, $21.9\%$), and cases requiring nonlocal recovery ($11/32$, $34.4\%$). Reference failures place the trajectory outside the bounded residual range; nonlocal cases require retreat, re-approach, or strategy switching after jamming or wrong-side contact. Together, these two categories account for $25/32$ failures and define the main boundary of local reference-anchored correction. The remaining staleness failures occur beyond the reference-age range used in training.

\subsection{Independent Residual Execution}
\label{sec:exp_runtime}

Two experiments assess independent residual execution. Runtime profiling quantifies the realized rate separation between the reference and residual paths; reference-delay evaluation tests whether this separation improves contact behavior as the cached reference becomes stale.

\noindent\textbf{Realized rate separation.}
We profile all paths on the button-pressing task using the deployment hardware (Table~\ref{tab:runtime}). The ForceDelta residual requires $2.43$~ms per isolated forward pass, compared with $908.69$~ms for the missing-force reference. With the two workers assigned to separate GPUs, the residual publishes at $120.40$~Hz, while the reference updates at $1.06$~Hz. The $100$-Hz command interface applies the latest valid residual packet at each control update; packet publication rate is therefore distinct from command application frequency and the action-sample rate within each packet. ImplicitRDP publishes reactive packets at $78.10$~Hz.

\noindent\textbf{Robustness to delayed references.}
We evaluate USB insertion and plug insertion with $0$, $100$, and $200$~ms of additional packet-publication delay, using $10$ trials per task, method, and delay. ForceDelta-VLA degrades more gradually than the Temporal Teacher: from $0$ to $200$~ms, success drops by $15$ versus $25$ percentage points, while peak contact force increases by approximately $30\%$ versus $60\%$. This suggests that independent residual updates improve robustness to delayed reference availability (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_sensitivity}).

\subsection{Ablation Studies}
\label{sec:exp_ablation}

We organize the ablations into two groups (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).
\emph{Execution/component ablations} keep the trained model fixed. \textbf{Reference only} disables both residual outputs; \textbf{w/o force residual} and \textbf{w/o staleness residual} disable the named output. \textbf{Shuffled force history} preserves the empirical force-history marginal but breaks its observation--action correspondence. \textbf{Zeroed residual input} replaces the deployed residual policy's measured force history with an all-zero tensor while retaining the force encoder and residual branch, suppressing measured force information but potentially creating an out-of-distribution input.

\emph{Supervision/decomposition ablations} retrain the affected variant. \textbf{Demonstration-derived residual target} subtracts the missing-force teacher prediction from the demonstrated action at the same query state. \textbf{Zero-wrench teacher query} replaces the learned missing-force mode during reference and target construction with an all-zero-wrench teacher query. \textbf{w/o schedule replay} omits sampled asynchronous query and packet-latency schedules during residual training. \textbf{Single combined-residual head} predicts the sum of the force and staleness targets with one head. \textbf{Fast full-action distillation} uses the same compact student inputs and update schedule but directly distills the frozen teacher's force-conditioned pose action. These interventions differ from $\pi_{0.5}$, a separately trained VLA with no force-input pathway.

Among the force-input diagnostics, shuffling produces the largest degradation: success falls from $85.0\%/75.0\%$ to $55.0\%/45.0\%$ on the single-arm/bimanual task groups, while peak force rises from $11.4/12.0$~N to $17.2/19.1$~N. Zeroing reduces success to $65.0\%/55.0\%$ and increases peak force to $15.4/16.8$~N. The larger shuffled-input drop reveals sensitivity to the alignment between force history, observation, and action.

The supervision/decomposition ablations locate the gain more precisely. A single combined head reduces success from $85.0\%/75.0\%$ to $75.0\%/65.0\%$ and raises peak force from $11.4/12.0$~N to $13.9/14.2$~N, showing the benefit of separate force-responsive and staleness heads. Full-action distillation into the same fast student reaches $67.5\%/52.5\%$ success with $15.1/16.6$~N peak force. Its $17.5/22.5$-point success gap to ForceDelta-VLA supports the roles of residual parameterization and cached-reference anchoring in high-rate execution. Demonstration-derived targets, the zero-wrench teacher query, and training without schedule replay also reduce success and increase peak force, supporting the contributions of teacher-defined deviation targets, the learned missing-force mode, and deployment-schedule replay.
