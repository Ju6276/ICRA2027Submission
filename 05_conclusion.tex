We presented ForceSplit, a teacher-guided framework for decomposing force-aware manipulation into slow nominal behavior and fast force-conditioned correction. The central hypothesis is that this division of responsibility should be learned from a unified temporal force-aware teacher rather than imposed only through the student architecture. By querying the same frozen teacher with observed force history and a learned null-force token, ForceSplit derives a force-unavailable nominal target and a complementary behavioral residual. These targets supervise a vision--language--state policy that produces nominal action chunks and semantic intent, and a lightweight force-conditioned policy that updates residual corrections at the robot control rate. The resulting students can be executed asynchronously without retaining the teacher at deployment.

Our experimental design evaluates whether this explicit decomposition preserves the teacher's task-level behavior while improving responsiveness and physical-interaction quality in contact-rich manipulation. In particular, the planned comparisons test the contributions of native-rate temporal force encoding, learned null-force conditioning, teacher-derived residual supervision, and asynchronous fast-policy execution. Beyond overall task success, the evaluation emphasizes contact-specific measures such as peak force, safety-limit violations, disturbance-recovery success, and correction latency.

Several limitations remain. The difference between full-force and null-force teacher predictions is a policy-space behavioral residual, not a guarantee of causal force attribution. Its usefulness also depends on the quality of the temporal teacher and on whether the learned null-force token represents force unavailability without introducing an out-of-distribution condition. Moreover, additive action correction may be insufficient for interactions that require discrete strategy changes, and asynchronous composition requires careful treatment of delay, stability, and safety constraints. Future work will investigate causal and uncertainty-aware decompositions, adaptive scheduling between the slow and fast policies, safety-constrained residual control, and evaluation across a broader range of robots, sensors, and contact-rich tasks.
