We presented FoRe-VLA, which decomposes a force-aware manipulation policy by \emph{attribution} rather than by architecture. Existing slow--fast force policies cache the vision--language prefix but keep an action generator in the high-rate loop, and therefore pay a full flow-matching denoise per update: $76$~ms, or $13$~Hz, on an RTX~4090. We observe that the high-rate branch need only produce the part of the action attributable to force, and obtain a label for that part by querying a frozen temporal force-aware teacher twice --- with the measured force window and with a learned null-force condition --- under a shared vision--language prefix and shared flow-matching noise. The resulting target is simple enough that a $44.7$~M student with a single deterministic forward pass suffices, reaching $417$~Hz, a $31.8\times$ reduction of the high-rate path. Randomizing the slow period, latency, and jitter while generating targets, and conditioning the student on its measured context age, makes one trained student deployable across rates it never saw.

Several limitations are worth stating plainly. Full-minus-null differencing is a conditional difference in policy space, not a causal force attribution: vision and state condition both branches and may carry contact cues, so we claim only that the deviation is the teacher's output change under full versus null force conditioning. The construction is also bounded above by the teacher; any imitation error the teacher makes is inherited, which is why no behavior-cloning term is placed on the student.

The most substantive limitation is that our supervision is entirely offline. Compliant Residual DAgger and LIFT both report that contact feedback induces a policy-dependent distribution shift which offline data cannot cover, and that on-policy corrective rollouts are important for contact-rich residual learning~\cite{xu2025crdagger,lv2026lift}. Our counterfactual target is drawn from demonstrations and inherits that limitation; the compensating advantage is that it requires no robot interaction and no human corrections to produce. Whether an offline counterfactual target is sufficient, or must be combined with a limited on-policy phase, is the question we consider most important for future work. Additive correction may also be inadequate for interactions requiring discrete strategy changes, and asynchronous composition demands explicit treatment of delay, stability, and safety limits. Finally, the latency and decomposition results reported here are offline; the task-level claims, and in particular whether $100$~Hz execution improves contact outcomes over $30$~Hz, remain to be established on hardware under the criteria of Sec.~\ref{sec:exp_criteria}.
