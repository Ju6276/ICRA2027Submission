Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have shown strong potential for general-purpose robotic manipulation, yet contact-rich tasks remain difficult because vision does not directly reveal interaction force, incipient slip, impact, or contact stability. Force-aware VLAs address this limitation by incorporating force/torque or tactile observations into action generation~\cite{yu2025forcevla,li2026forcevla2,huang2026tafvla}, making physical feedback available to the policy where it is essential for precise and safe interaction.

Providing force as an input does not by itself guarantee timely reaction. Vision--language inference is comparatively expensive, whereas contact states can change within a single control cycle. A now-standard response is to separate the policy by rate: a slow vision--language backbone updates a reusable context at low frequency, and a high-rate branch consumes that cached context together with the newest force measurements~\cite{xue2025rdp,li2026favla,vanjani2026damvla,li2026atvla,niu2026trex,wang2026phaforce,lv2026lift,fisvla2025}. This deployment structure is sound and we adopt it.

The high-rate branch in these systems, however, remains a full action generator. FAVLA re-runs the $\pi_0$ action expert against a cached key/value context~\cite{li2026favla,black2024pi0}, and LIFT refreshes a reactive action expert within the chunk against a cached vision--language prefix~\cite{lv2026lift}. Caching eliminates the vision--language forward pass but not the cost of action generation: each high-rate update still performs a complete flow-matching denoise. The resulting update period is an order of magnitude longer than the sampling period of a contemporary force sensor, and Sec.~\ref{sec:exp_latency} shows that even a single denoising step consumes most of a $100$~Hz budget. The rate gap is therefore structural rather than an artifact of implementation.

The premise of this work is that the high-rate branch need not reproduce the action. Writing the command as a reference plus a correction, only the correction must be recomputed when new force arrives, since the reference evolves on the semantic timescale and can be interpolated from the most recent slow update. Our hypothesis is that this correction is a smoother and lower-dimensional quantity than a full action chunk, and that a correspondingly smaller network is sufficient to represent it. What prevents the decomposition from being learned directly is supervision: demonstrations contain the total action and synchronized force, but no label for the force-attributable component. Supervising both terms against the same total action leaves the decomposition underdetermined, permitting the reference to absorb contact-dependent behavior while the correction redundantly reproduces nominal motion.

We construct the missing label from a frozen teacher. A temporal force-aware teacher is first trained on synchronized vision, language, state, and native-rate force history, then queried twice for the same observation, once with the measured force window and once with a learned null-force condition. Two properties make the difference of these outputs a meaningful target. First, the two queries share both the vision--language prefix and the flow-matching noise draw, so the force condition is the only quantity that differs; under independent noise the difference is dominated by sampling variance. Second, the null condition is a learned token adapted on the frozen teacher rather than a zero-padded input, so the null branch remains a competent force-free mode of the network instead of an out-of-distribution query whose output change would reflect degradation rather than force. The null-conditioned chunk additionally serves as the slow reference, so no separate nominal policy is distilled.

Differencing a conditional model under a removed condition is itself established. Classifier-free guidance forms this difference at inference time to amplify conditioning~\cite{ho2022cfg}, and guidance distillation compresses the guided combination into a cheaper student~\cite{meng2023guidancedistill}. In embodied settings, counterfactual modality interventions have been used to attribute predictions to modalities~\cite{cma2026modality,embodiedinterp2026}, to deconfound visual shortcuts~\cite{cofactvla2026}, and to reweight actions at test time~\cite{idr2026causal}. These methods interpret, debias, or adjust a single model operating at a single rate. We instead use the difference as a supervision target that factorizes a policy across time scales.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a decomposition of a force-aware policy by attribution rather than by architecture, in which the high-rate branch is supervised by the teacher's force-attributable deviation instead of the full action, so that action generation in that branch is replaced by a single forward pass of a small network;
    \item a counterfactual construction for this target, combining a learned null-force condition adapted on a frozen teacher with shared-prefix and shared-noise queries, which separates the effect of the force condition from sampling variance and from the degradation induced by a zero-padded intervention;
    \item a rate-agnostic training scheme in which the slow update period, inference latency, and jitter are randomized during target generation while the student is conditioned on its measured context age.
\end{enumerate}

We evaluate on eight contact-rich single-arm and bimanual manipulation tasks against $\pi_{0.5}$~\cite{black2025pi05}, ForceVLA~\cite{yu2025forcevla}, and TA-VLA~\cite{zhang2025tavla}, and report the attainable rate of the high-rate path, the task and interaction metrics, and an analysis of the decomposition itself.
