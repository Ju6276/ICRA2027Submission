Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models provide a scalable interface between semantic instructions, visual observations, and robot actions. Their performance nevertheless degrades in contact-intensive tasks, where images alone cannot reliably reveal contact onset, incipient slip, impact, or excessive load. Recent force-aware VLAs address this observability gap by conditioning action generation on force/torque or tactile measurements~\cite{yu2025forcevla,li2026forcevla2,huang2026tafvla}. The remaining challenge is temporal: force sensors commonly update much faster than the multimodal policy that consumes them.

Slow--fast architectures mitigate this mismatch by evaluating an expensive visual--language backbone intermittently and reusing its context in a reactive branch~\cite{xue2025rdp,li2026favla,vanjani2026damvla,li2026atvla,niu2026trex,wang2026phaforce,lv2026lift}. However, the fast branch typically remains a full action generator. In flow-matching VLAs, every reactive update therefore requires multiple passes through a large action expert even when the vision--language prefix is cached. Our measurements show that this computation, rather than perception, prevents the reactive path from matching a $100$~Hz force stream.

We ask whether the fast branch must regenerate the entire action. Semantic intent and coarse motion evolve at the slower visual--language timescale, whereas newly arriving force primarily calls for a local correction. This suggests a decomposition into a slowly updated reference and a high-rate residual. Demonstrations provide only their sum, however, so ordinary behavior cloning does not determine which behavior belongs to either branch. Training a small network directly on demonstrated actions may simply recreate nominal motion rather than specialize in force-driven correction.

ForceRes-VLA obtains explicit residual supervision from paired queries of a frozen temporal force-aware teacher. For the same visual observation, instruction, and robot state, we query the teacher with either the measured force history or an adapted null-force condition. Both queries share the visual--language prefix and initial flow noise, suppressing nuisance variation in their difference. Because null mode includes a learned token and a null-specific low-rank adapter, the difference is not a causal or uniquely force-attributable component; it is the teacher's \emph{conditional policy deviation} between its full and null modes. This deviation trains a lightweight residual student, while the same teacher in null mode supplies the slow reference. The student consumes native-rate force history and executes asynchronously without rerunning a generative action expert.

To tolerate realistic scheduling, we randomize slow-policy period, inference latency, and jitter during training and condition the student on the age of its cached context. We evaluate both whether the paired target is structured around contact and whether reproducing it at high rate improves physical outcomes. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a paired full/null teacher construction that provides explicit conditional-residual targets while controlling flow-sampling variation;
    \item a residual-only fast policy that combines native-rate force history with a slow teacher reference and replaces iterative action generation with one deterministic forward pass; and
    \item timing-randomized training and asynchronous execution for a single residual policy across slow-branch rates and latencies.
\end{enumerate}

Across eight single-arm and bimanual tasks, our evaluation compares task success, force safety, recovery behavior, residual fidelity, and execution cost against force-free, force-aware, and slow--fast baselines. The measured residual path runs in $2.4$~ms on an RTX~4090, leaving the robot experiments to determine whether this computational advantage translates into improved contact behavior.
